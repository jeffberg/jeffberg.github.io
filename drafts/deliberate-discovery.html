Deliberate Discovery

Principled Exploration Under Parallactic Constraint
Jeffrey J. Berg · PARALLAX™ · CONSTELLATION™ · Paper 7 of 10 · Target: PNAS

“The voyage of discovery consists not in seeking new landscapes, but in having new eyes.”

— Marcel Proust, In Search of Lost Time (c. 1913)

A Note Before the Dive

Discovery is not accident. It is structured attention — the deliberate choice of where to look, with what instrument, at what resolution. The replication crisis taught science that undisciplined exploration produces false positives at scale. The parallax framework offers a remedy: explore freely, but require that any discovered pattern be visible from more than one analytical lens before it is trusted.

This paper demonstrates deliberate discovery — principled exploration that generates hypotheses from one view and immediately tests them against another. The constraint is not conservatism. It is rigor. Six scenarios show the method in action: anomalies that survive cross-validation, hypotheses that data arbitrate without analyst bias, sweeps that separate robust structure from configuration artifacts, cross-domain predictions that test whether physics forecasts biology, replication across spatial partitions, and the deliberate pointing of instruments at the places no one has looked.

The ocean was chosen because it is legible, public, and patient. The method was engineered for systems where discovery is harder to distinguish from hallucination.

Scenario I — The Anomaly Hunt

Where in the ocean does temperature behave unexpectedly — and does the unexpected pattern survive when checked against an independent data source?

-- Generate anomaly candidates from subsurface profiles, -- then cross-validate against satellite SST WITH anomaly_candidates AS ( SELECT ROUND(lat / 5) * 5 AS lat_bin, ROUND(lon / 5) * 5 AS lon_bin, AVG(temperature_c) AS mean_temp, STDDEV(temperature_c) AS sd_temp, COUNT(*) AS n_profiles FROM argo.float_profiles WHERE pressure_dbar BETWEEN 50 AND 500 AND quality_flag IN (1, 2) AND observation_ts >= '2018-01-01' GROUP BY lat_bin, lon_bin HAVING COUNT(*) >= 30 ), satellite_check AS ( SELECT ROUND(lat / 5) * 5 AS lat_bin, ROUND(lon / 5) * 5 AS lon_bin, AVG(sst_anomaly_c) AS sat_anomaly, STDDEV(sst_anomaly_c) AS sat_sd FROM noaa.sea_surface_temperature WHERE observation_date >= '2018-01-01' GROUP BY lat_bin, lon_bin HAVING COUNT(*) >= 50 ) SELECT a.lat_bin, a.lon_bin, ROUND(a.mean_temp::numeric, 2) AS argo_mean_c, ROUND(a.sd_temp::numeric, 3) AS argo_sd, ROUND(s.sat_anomaly::numeric, 3) AS satellite_anomaly, ROUND( ABS(a.mean_temp - AVG(a.mean_temp) OVER ()) / NULLIF(a.sd_temp, 0)::numeric, 2 ) AS argo_z_score, CASE WHEN ABS(a.mean_temp - AVG(a.mean_temp) OVER ()) / NULLIF(a.sd_temp, 0) > 2 AND ABS(s.sat_anomaly) / NULLIF(s.sat_sd, 0) > 2 THEN 'parallactic discovery' WHEN ABS(a.mean_temp - AVG(a.mean_temp) OVER ()) / NULLIF(a.sd_temp, 0) > 2 THEN 'monoptic artifact' ELSE 'within expectations' END AS discovery_verdict FROM anomaly_candidates a LEFT JOIN satellite_check s USING (lat_bin, lon_bin) ORDER BY argo_z_score DESC LIMIT 30;
The anomaly hunt is the oldest form of exploration — and the most dangerous. Without cross-validation, every outlier looks like a discovery. This query generates candidates from one instrument (Argo subsurface floats) and immediately tests each against an independent source (NOAA satellite SST). The verdict is ternary: patterns visible from both views are parallactic discoveries; patterns visible from only one are monoptic artifacts; everything else is noise. The discipline is in the immediate check, not in the original detection.

Scenario II — The Hypothesis Fork

When an exploratory analysis suggests two competing explanations, can the data itself arbitrate — without the analyst choosing favorites?

-- Fork: is regional SST rise a secular warming trend -- or an El Niño artifact? Let residuals decide. WITH regional_trends AS ( SELECT ROUND(lat / 10) * 10 AS lat_band, ROUND(lon / 10) * 10 AS lon_band, EXTRACT(YEAR FROM observation_date) AS yr, AVG(sst_anomaly_c) AS mean_anomaly, COUNT(*) AS n_obs FROM noaa.sea_surface_temperature WHERE observation_date BETWEEN '2000-01-01' AND '2024-12-31' GROUP BY lat_band, lon_band, yr HAVING COUNT(*) >= 50 ), hypothesis_fork AS ( SELECT lat_band, lon_band, -- Hypothesis A: linear warming trend REGR_SLOPE(mean_anomaly, yr) AS linear_slope, REGR_R2(mean_anomaly, yr) AS linear_r2, -- Hypothesis B: El Niño cycle (peaks near 2015-16, 2023-24) CORR(mean_anomaly, CASE WHEN yr IN (2002,2004,2009,2015,2016,2023,2024) THEN 1.0 ELSE 0.0 END) AS enso_corr, POWER(CORR(mean_anomaly, CASE WHEN yr IN (2002,2004,2009,2015,2016,2023,2024) THEN 1.0 ELSE 0.0 END), 2) AS enso_r2, COUNT(*) AS n_years FROM regional_trends GROUP BY lat_band, lon_band HAVING COUNT(*) >= 15 ) SELECT lat_band, lon_band, ROUND(linear_slope::numeric, 5) AS warming_slope, ROUND(linear_r2::numeric, 3) AS trend_fit, ROUND(enso_corr::numeric, 3) AS enso_correlation, ROUND(enso_r2::numeric, 3) AS enso_fit, CASE WHEN linear_r2 > enso_r2 * 1.5 THEN 'data favors warming trend' WHEN enso_r2 > linear_r2 * 1.5 THEN 'data favors ENSO artifact' ELSE 'models indistinguishable' END AS arbitration FROM hypothesis_fork ORDER BY ABS(linear_r2 - enso_r2) DESC LIMIT 30;
The hypothesis fork is the antidote to confirmation bias. When exploration surfaces a pattern — rising SST anomalies in a region — the analyst’s instinct is to pick the most exciting explanation. This query refuses. It fits two competing models to the same data (linear warming vs. ENSO cycling), compares their residuals, and lets the fit statistics arbitrate per region. Some regions will show secular warming. Others will show cyclic behavior masquerading as trend. The analyst learns which explanation the data prefer — not which explanation the analyst preferred before looking.

Scenario III — The Structured Sweep

If we systematically vary the analytical lens — resolution, time window, variable — how many distinct patterns emerge, and which survive across multiple configurations?

-- 9 configurations: 3 resolutions x 3 time windows -- Count which thermal patterns survive across configs WITH configuration_sweep AS ( SELECT res.bin_size, tw.start_year, tw.end_year, ROUND(fp.lat / res.bin_size) * res.bin_size AS lat_bin, ROUND(fp.lon / res.bin_size) * res.bin_size AS lon_bin, AVG(fp.temperature_c) AS mean_temp, STDDEV(fp.temperature_c) AS sd_temp, COUNT(*) AS n_obs FROM argo.float_profiles fp CROSS JOIN ( VALUES (2), (5), (10) ) AS res(bin_size) CROSS JOIN ( VALUES (2015, 2018), (2018, 2021), (2021, 2024) ) AS tw(start_year, end_year) WHERE fp.quality_flag IN (1, 2) AND fp.pressure_dbar BETWEEN 100 AND 500 AND EXTRACT(YEAR FROM fp.observation_ts) BETWEEN tw.start_year AND tw.end_year GROUP BY res.bin_size, tw.start_year, tw.end_year, lat_bin, lon_bin HAVING COUNT(*) >= 20 ), anomaly_flags AS ( SELECT lat_bin, lon_bin, bin_size, start_year, CASE WHEN ABS(mean_temp - AVG(mean_temp) OVER (PARTITION BY bin_size, start_year)) / NULLIF(sd_temp, 0) > 1.5 THEN 1 ELSE 0 END AS is_anomaly FROM configuration_sweep ) SELECT lat_bin, lon_bin, SUM(is_anomaly) AS configs_flagged, COUNT(*) AS configs_tested, ROUND(100.0 * SUM(is_anomaly) / COUNT(*), 1) AS pct_flagged, CASE WHEN SUM(is_anomaly) >= 5 THEN 'robust discovery' WHEN SUM(is_anomaly) >= 3 THEN 'partial stability' WHEN SUM(is_anomaly) = 1 THEN 'configuration artifact' ELSE 'no anomaly detected' END AS robustness_class FROM anomaly_flags GROUP BY lat_bin, lon_bin HAVING SUM(is_anomaly) >= 1 ORDER BY configs_flagged DESC, pct_flagged DESC LIMIT 25;
Most analyses run once, at one resolution, over one time window. The structured sweep refuses this convenience. Nine configurations — three spatial resolutions crossed with three temporal windows — produce nine independent views of the same ocean. A pattern that appears in one configuration is a curiosity. A pattern that appears in five or more is a robust discovery. A pattern that appears in exactly one is a configuration artifact — an analytical ghost created by the specific combination of resolution and window. The sweep does not discover patterns. It discovers which patterns deserve to be called discoveries.

Scenario IV — The Cross-Domain Surprise

Can a pattern discovered in physical oceanography — temperature, salinity — predict something in biological oceanography — and is this predictive power a genuine discovery or an ecological truism?

-- Do thermohaline anomalies predict biodiversity anomalies? -- Measure surprise: cross-domain signal vs within-domain baseline WITH thermohaline_regions AS ( SELECT ROUND(lat / 5) * 5 AS lat_bin, ROUND(lon / 5) * 5 AS lon_bin, AVG(temperature_c) AS mean_temp, AVG(salinity_psu) AS mean_sal, STDDEV(temperature_c) AS temp_var, ABS(AVG(temperature_c) - AVG(AVG(temperature_c)) OVER ()) / NULLIF(STDDEV(AVG(temperature_c)) OVER (), 0) AS thermal_z FROM argo.float_profiles WHERE pressure_dbar BETWEEN 10 AND 200 AND quality_flag IN (1, 2) GROUP BY lat_bin, lon_bin HAVING COUNT(*) >= 30 ), cross_domain_surprise AS ( SELECT t.lat_bin, t.lon_bin, t.thermal_z, COUNT(DISTINCT b.species_name) AS species_richness, AVG(b.abundance) AS mean_abundance, COUNT(*) AS n_bio_obs FROM thermohaline_regions t JOIN obis.biodiversity_observations b ON ROUND(b.lat / 5) * 5 = t.lat_bin AND ROUND(b.lon / 5) * 5 = t.lon_bin GROUP BY t.lat_bin, t.lon_bin, t.thermal_z HAVING COUNT(*) >= 10 ) SELECT lat_bin, lon_bin, ROUND(thermal_z::numeric, 2) AS thermal_anomaly_z, species_richness, ROUND(mean_abundance::numeric, 1) AS mean_abundance, ROUND(species_richness::numeric / NULLIF( AVG(species_richness) OVER (), 0), 2) AS richness_ratio, CASE WHEN thermal_z > 1.5 AND species_richness > AVG(species_richness) OVER () * 1.3 THEN 'cross-domain discovery' WHEN thermal_z > 1.5 THEN 'physical anomaly only' WHEN species_richness > AVG(species_richness) OVER () * 1.3 THEN 'biological anomaly only' ELSE 'no cross-domain signal' END AS surprise_class FROM cross_domain_surprise ORDER BY thermal_z DESC LIMIT 25;
The most powerful discoveries cross domain boundaries. A thermal anomaly is interesting. A thermal anomaly that predicts elevated biodiversity is a discovery. But the test must be honest: is the cross-domain signal genuinely surprising, or is it the well-known relationship between warm, stratified water and tropical species richness? The richness ratio provides the baseline. Where the ratio exceeds expectation in regions of thermal anomaly, the cross-domain link tells us something new. Where it merely recapitulates the latitude gradient, it tells us something we already knew. Deliberate discovery means knowing the difference.

Scenario V — The Replication Test

If we split the ocean in half — by hemisphere — does a discovered pattern replicate in the held-out half?

-- Spatial cross-validation: discover in Northern Hemisphere, -- replicate in Southern Hemisphere WITH northern_discovery AS ( SELECT ROUND(lon / 10) * 10 AS lon_band, EXTRACT(MONTH FROM observation_date) AS month, AVG(sst_anomaly_c) AS nh_anomaly, STDDEV(sst_anomaly_c) AS nh_sd, REGR_SLOPE(sst_anomaly_c, EXTRACT(YEAR FROM observation_date)) AS nh_trend, COUNT(*) AS nh_n FROM noaa.sea_surface_temperature WHERE lat > 0 AND observation_date >= '2005-01-01' GROUP BY lon_band, month HAVING COUNT(*) >= 100 ), replication_test AS ( SELECT ROUND(lon / 10) * 10 AS lon_band, EXTRACT(MONTH FROM observation_date) AS month, AVG(sst_anomaly_c) AS sh_anomaly, STDDEV(sst_anomaly_c) AS sh_sd, REGR_SLOPE(sst_anomaly_c, EXTRACT(YEAR FROM observation_date)) AS sh_trend, COUNT(*) AS sh_n FROM noaa.sea_surface_temperature WHERE lat < 0 AND observation_date >= '2005-01-01' GROUP BY lon_band, month HAVING COUNT(*) >= 100 ) SELECT n.lon_band, n.month, ROUND(n.nh_trend::numeric, 5) AS nh_trend_slope, ROUND(s.sh_trend::numeric, 5) AS sh_trend_slope, ROUND(ABS(n.nh_trend - s.sh_trend)::numeric, 5) AS trend_divergence, ROUND((SIGN(n.nh_trend) * SIGN(s.sh_trend)) ::numeric, 0) AS sign_agreement, CASE WHEN SIGN(n.nh_trend) = SIGN(s.sh_trend) AND ABS(n.nh_trend - s.sh_trend) < GREATEST(ABS(n.nh_trend), ABS(s.sh_trend)) * 0.5 THEN 'replicates' WHEN SIGN(n.nh_trend) = SIGN(s.sh_trend) THEN 'partial replication' ELSE 'fails to replicate' END AS replication_verdict FROM northern_discovery n JOIN replication_test s USING (lon_band, month) ORDER BY trend_divergence ASC LIMIT 30;
The replication crisis was a crisis of geography: findings discovered in one sample failed to appear in another. This query applies the same logic spatially. Patterns discovered in the Northern Hemisphere are tested for replication in the Southern Hemisphere. The split is not random — it is the most adversarial partition available, because the hemispheres differ in land mass distribution, ocean circulation, and seasonal phase. A trend that replicates across this divide is robust. A trend that reverses sign is hemisphere-specific. The parallactic version of cross-validation does not shuffle rows. It splits the world in half and asks: does this survive?

Scenario VI — The Deliberate Blank

Where has no one looked — and does pointing instruments at the unobserved reveal structure that the observed regions predicted, or something entirely new?

-- Find the ocean's blind spots, then test whether -- the few observations from blanks follow or break predictions WITH observed_coverage AS ( SELECT ROUND(deploy_lat / 10) * 10 AS lat_bin, ROUND(deploy_lon / 10) * 10 AS lon_bin, COUNT(DISTINCT float_id) AS n_floats FROM argo.float_metadata GROUP BY lat_bin, lon_bin ), deliberate_blank AS ( SELECT g.lat AS lat_bin, g.lon AS lon_bin, g.depth_m AS bathy_depth, g.feature_type, COALESCE(oc.n_floats, 0) AS n_floats, CASE WHEN COALESCE(oc.n_floats, 0) = 0 THEN 'unobserved' WHEN oc.n_floats < 3 THEN 'sparsely observed' ELSE 'well observed' END AS coverage_class FROM gebco.bathymetry g LEFT JOIN observed_coverage oc ON ROUND(g.lat / 10) * 10 = oc.lat_bin AND ROUND(g.lon / 10) * 10 = oc.lon_bin WHERE g.depth_m < -200 -- ocean only ), blank_biodiversity AS ( SELECT db.coverage_class, db.feature_type, COUNT(DISTINCT b.species_name) AS species_found, AVG(b.abundance) AS mean_abundance, COUNT(*) AS n_bio_obs FROM deliberate_blank db JOIN obis.biodiversity_observations b ON ROUND(b.lat / 10) * 10 = db.lat_bin AND ROUND(b.lon / 10) * 10 = db.lon_bin GROUP BY db.coverage_class, db.feature_type ) SELECT coverage_class, feature_type, species_found, ROUND(mean_abundance::numeric, 1) AS mean_abundance, n_bio_obs, ROUND(species_found::numeric / NULLIF( AVG(species_found) OVER ( PARTITION BY feature_type), 0), 2) AS vs_observed_baseline, CASE WHEN species_found > AVG(species_found) OVER (PARTITION BY feature_type) * 1.2 THEN 'exceeds prediction' WHEN species_found < AVG(species_found) OVER (PARTITION BY feature_type) * 0.8 THEN 'below prediction' ELSE 'matches prediction' END AS blank_verdict FROM blank_biodiversity ORDER BY coverage_class, feature_type;
The deliberate blank is the hardest test of discovery because it asks the question that observation-biased science avoids: what exists where we have not looked? This query identifies ocean regions with zero or near-zero Argo deployments, compares their bathymetric features to well-observed regions, and then tests whether the sparse biological observations available from these blanks follow the patterns predicted by observed regions — or diverge. Where they match, the observed world generalizes. Where they diverge, the blank has taught us something the instrumented ocean could not. The blank is not the absence of data. It is the ultimate test of whether our discoveries are universal or local.

Coda

Six modes of discovery. One ocean as proving ground.

The anomaly hunt generates candidates and immediately cross-validates them against an independent source. The hypothesis fork lets data arbitrate between competing explanations without the analyst placing a thumb on the scale. The structured sweep separates robust patterns from configuration artifacts by systematically varying resolution, time window, and threshold. The cross-domain surprise tests whether physics predicts biology — and whether that prediction is genuinely informative or merely recapitulates what latitude already told us. The replication test holds discovered patterns to the standard of reproducibility by splitting the world along its most adversarial boundary. The deliberate blank asks the question that observation-biased science refuses to ask: what happens when we point our instruments at the places no one has looked?

Deliberate discovery is neither undisciplined exploration nor rigid confirmation. It is the principled middle path: explore freely, but trust only what survives the parallactic test. The freedom to explore is real. The constraint on what counts as found is severe. This asymmetry is the method.

The replication crisis taught science that exploration without structure produces false positives at scale. Rigid confirmatory analysis taught science that structure without exploration produces stale hypotheses that miss the world’s novelty. The parallax framework resolves the tension: generate from one view, test from another. What survives the crossing is discovered. What does not is discarded without regret.

What the lenses find depends entirely on where they are pointed next.
