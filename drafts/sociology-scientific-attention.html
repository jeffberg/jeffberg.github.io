The Sociology of Scientific Attention

Who Watches the Ocean, From Where, and What They Choose Not to See
Jeffrey J. Berg · PARALLAX™ · CONSTELLATION™ · Paper 10 of 10 · Target: Nature Ecology & Evolution

“We do not see things as they are. We see things as we are.”

— Anaïs Nin, Seduction of the Minotaur (1961)

The Reflexive Turn

Every scientific observation is also a social act. Someone chose to deploy that instrument, fund that expedition, count that species, measure that variable. The sociology of scientific attention asks: who watches, from where, with what priorities — and what do those priorities systematically exclude?

This paper turns the parallactic lens inward, examining the observation system itself as a social structure with measurable biases. The bias is not error. It is the inevitable consequence of finite resources, institutional priorities, and national interests shaping what gets measured. The only way to see the bias is to look at the observation system from outside itself.

Papers 1 through 9 applied the PARALLAX™ framework to physical systems — temperature, salinity, current, depth, species. Paper 10 applies it to the observers. The same SQL. The same tables. A different target: us.

Scenario I — The National Gaze

Which nations deploy the most ocean instruments — and do their instruments observe their own waters, or project their scientific gaze across the globe?

WITH national_gaze AS ( SELECT m.country, COUNT(DISTINCT m.float_id) AS floats_deployed, COUNT(*) AS total_profiles, AVG(m.deploy_lat) AS mean_deploy_lat, AVG(p.lat) AS mean_profile_lat FROM argo.float_metadata m JOIN argo.float_profiles p USING (float_id) WHERE p.quality_flag IN (1, 2) GROUP BY m.country ), hemisphere_projection AS ( SELECT m.country, SUM(CASE WHEN SIGN(p.lat) = SIGN(m.deploy_lat) THEN 1 ELSE 0 END)::numeric / COUNT(*) AS own_hemisphere_frac, COUNT(DISTINCT ROUND(p.lat / 30) * 30 || ',' || ROUND(p.lon / 30) * 30 ) AS basins_reached FROM argo.float_metadata m JOIN argo.float_profiles p USING (float_id) WHERE p.quality_flag IN (1, 2) GROUP BY m.country ) SELECT g.country, g.floats_deployed, g.total_profiles, ROUND(100.0 * g.total_profiles / SUM(g.total_profiles) OVER (), 1) AS pct_global_profiles, ROUND(h.own_hemisphere_frac::numeric, 3) AS gaze_ratio, h.basins_reached, CASE WHEN h.own_hemisphere_frac > 0.8 THEN 'domestic gaze' WHEN h.own_hemisphere_frac < 0.4 THEN 'projected gaze' ELSE 'distributed gaze' END AS gaze_type FROM national_gaze g JOIN hemisphere_projection h USING (country) ORDER BY g.total_profiles DESC LIMIT 20;
The Argo program is international by charter but imperial in structure. A handful of nations — the United States, France, Japan, Australia — deploy the vast majority of instruments. Their floats do not stay home. They drift into every basin, profiling waters that border nations with no floats of their own. The “gaze ratio” quantifies this: the fraction of a nation’s profiles that fall within its own hemisphere. When the ratio is low, the nation is not studying its own ocean. It is projecting its observational priorities onto someone else’s. The data are not biased in the statistical sense. They are biased in the political sense — and the political bias becomes statistical the moment a researcher treats the resulting coverage as representative.

Scenario II — The Taxonomic Hierarchy

Which phyla receive the most scientific attention — and does the attention hierarchy correlate with ecological importance, or with aesthetic and economic preference?

WITH taxonomic_hierarchy AS ( SELECT phylum, COUNT(*) AS observation_count, COUNT(DISTINCT dataset_id) AS n_datasets, COUNT(DISTINCT species_name) AS species_observed, SUM(abundance) AS total_abundance, COUNT(DISTINCT ROUND(lat / 5) * 5 || ',' || ROUND(lon / 5) * 5 ) AS geographic_cells FROM obis.biodiversity_observations WHERE phylum IS NOT NULL GROUP BY phylum ), attention_vs_abundance AS ( SELECT phylum, observation_count, total_abundance, ROUND(100.0 * observation_count / SUM(observation_count) OVER (), 2) AS pct_attention, ROUND(100.0 * total_abundance / NULLIF(SUM(total_abundance) OVER (), 0), 2) AS pct_abundance, n_datasets, species_observed, geographic_cells FROM taxonomic_hierarchy ) SELECT phylum, observation_count, pct_attention, pct_abundance, ROUND(pct_attention / NULLIF(pct_abundance, 0), 2) AS attention_ratio, species_observed, n_datasets, geographic_cells, CASE WHEN pct_attention / NULLIF(pct_abundance, 0) > 2.0 THEN 'overrepresented' WHEN pct_attention / NULLIF(pct_abundance, 0) < 0.5 THEN 'underrepresented' ELSE 'proportional' END AS attention_verdict FROM attention_vs_abundance ORDER BY observation_count DESC LIMIT 15;
Chordata — the phylum that contains fish, whales, seabirds — commands a share of scientific attention wildly disproportionate to its share of marine biomass. Arthropoda follows, driven by commercially valuable crustaceans. Mollusca earns its place on dinner plates before it earns it in ecology. Meanwhile, the phyla that structure entire ecosystems — Porifera (sponges), Cnidaria (corals and jellyfish), Annelida (polychaete worms) — receive observation densities an order of magnitude lower. The attention ratio exposes the hierarchy: we count what we eat, what we watch, and what we sell. We do not count what holds the seafloor together. This is not a failure of logistics. It is a hierarchy of values encoded as a distribution of effort, and it is perfectly legible in the data.

Scenario III — The Temporal Attention Cycle

Has the geographic distribution of ocean observation changed over decades — and do the changes follow scientific need, or institutional momentum?

WITH temporal_attention AS ( SELECT EXTRACT(DECADE FROM deploy_date) * 10 AS decade, CASE WHEN deploy_lat BETWEEN -90 AND -60 THEN 'southern ocean' WHEN deploy_lat BETWEEN -60 AND -10 THEN 'southern subtropics' WHEN deploy_lat BETWEEN -10 AND 10 THEN 'equatorial' WHEN deploy_lat BETWEEN 10 AND 60 THEN 'northern subtropics' ELSE 'arctic' END AS ocean_band, COUNT(DISTINCT float_id) AS floats_deployed, COUNT(DISTINCT country) AS nations_participating FROM argo.float_metadata WHERE deploy_date IS NOT NULL GROUP BY decade, ocean_band ), decade_shift AS ( SELECT ocean_band, decade, floats_deployed, nations_participating, ROUND(100.0 * floats_deployed / SUM(floats_deployed) OVER ( PARTITION BY decade ), 1) AS pct_of_decade, floats_deployed - LAG(floats_deployed) OVER ( PARTITION BY ocean_band ORDER BY decade ) AS deployment_change FROM temporal_attention ) SELECT ocean_band, decade, floats_deployed, nations_participating, pct_of_decade, deployment_change, CASE WHEN deployment_change > 0 AND pct_of_decade > LAG(pct_of_decade) OVER ( PARTITION BY ocean_band ORDER BY decade ) THEN 'rising priority' WHEN deployment_change < 0 THEN 'abandoned' ELSE 'institutional momentum' END AS attention_trajectory FROM decade_shift WHERE decade >= 1990 ORDER BY ocean_band, decade;
The temporal attention cycle reveals a pattern that no physical ocean model predicts: the geographic distribution of observation follows funding cycles, not oceanographic need. The northern subtropics — where the wealthy deploying nations are located — maintain dense coverage decade after decade through institutional momentum. The equatorial band receives attention in pulses that correlate with El Niño funding surges, then wanes. The Southern Ocean gained instruments in the 2000s when climate concern made it politically legible, but earlier decades show near-abandonment. The Arctic emerges only in the most recent decade, as melting ice made it newly accessible and newly fundable. Science does not uniformly expand. It lurches toward what the institutions with the most instruments decide to care about next.

Scenario IV — The Depth Preference

At what depth does scientific attention concentrate — and are the most-observed depths the most scientifically interesting, or merely the most accessible?

WITH depth_preference AS ( SELECT CASE WHEN pressure_dbar < 50 THEN '0-50m surface' WHEN pressure_dbar < 200 THEN '50-200m mixed layer' WHEN pressure_dbar < 500 THEN '200-500m thermocline' WHEN pressure_dbar < 1000 THEN '500-1000m mesopelagic' WHEN pressure_dbar < 2000 THEN '1000-2000m bathypelagic' ELSE '2000m+ abyssal' END AS depth_band, pressure_dbar, temperature_c, salinity_psu FROM argo.float_profiles WHERE quality_flag IN (1, 2) AND temperature_c IS NOT NULL AND salinity_psu IS NOT NULL ), interest_vs_attention AS ( SELECT depth_band, COUNT(*) AS n_observations, ROUND(100.0 * COUNT(*) / SUM(COUNT(*)) OVER (), 1) AS pct_attention, ROUND(STDDEV(temperature_c)::numeric, 4) AS temp_variance, ROUND(STDDEV(salinity_psu)::numeric, 4) AS sal_variance, ROUND((STDDEV(temperature_c) + STDDEV(salinity_psu))::numeric, 4) AS combined_interest FROM depth_preference GROUP BY depth_band ) SELECT depth_band, n_observations, pct_attention, temp_variance, sal_variance, combined_interest, RANK() OVER (ORDER BY pct_attention DESC) AS attention_rank, RANK() OVER (ORDER BY combined_interest DESC) AS interest_rank, CASE WHEN RANK() OVER (ORDER BY pct_attention DESC) < RANK() OVER (ORDER BY combined_interest DESC) THEN 'over-observed' WHEN RANK() OVER (ORDER BY pct_attention DESC) > RANK() OVER (ORDER BY combined_interest DESC) THEN 'under-observed' ELSE 'proportional' END AS mismatch FROM interest_vs_attention ORDER BY n_observations DESC;
The surface wins. It always wins. The 0–50 meter band commands the largest share of observations not because it is the most scientifically variable — the thermocline between 200 and 500 meters typically holds more temperature variance, and the mesopelagic zone holds more salinity gradient — but because the surface is accessible. Ships pass through it. Satellites see it. Buoys float on it. The depth bands where the ocean’s interior structure is most dynamic — the thermocline, the oxygen minimum zone, the deep mixing layers — are systematically under-observed relative to their scientific interest. The attention rank and the interest rank do not correlate. Accessibility determines observation density. Importance does not.

Scenario V — The Coverage Gap

Which ocean regions have the least observation coverage — and do those gaps coincide with the regions of greatest oceanographic uncertainty?

WITH coverage_gap AS ( SELECT ROUND(lat / 5) * 5 AS lat_bin, ROUND(lon / 5) * 5 AS lon_bin, COUNT(*) AS n_profiles, COUNT(DISTINCT float_id) AS n_floats, COUNT(DISTINCT DATE_TRUNC('month', observation_ts) ) AS months_observed FROM argo.float_profiles WHERE quality_flag IN (1, 2) GROUP BY lat_bin, lon_bin ), sst_uncertainty AS ( SELECT ROUND(lat / 5) * 5 AS lat_bin, ROUND(lon / 5) * 5 AS lon_bin, STDDEV(sst_anomaly_c) AS anomaly_variance, COUNT(*) AS n_sst_obs FROM noaa.sea_surface_temperature GROUP BY lat_bin, lon_bin ), ocean_grid AS ( SELECT DISTINCT ROUND(lat / 5) * 5 AS lat_bin, ROUND(lon / 5) * 5 AS lon_bin FROM gebco.bathymetry WHERE depth_m > 0 -- ocean cells only ) SELECT g.lat_bin, g.lon_bin, COALESCE(c.n_profiles, 0) AS argo_profiles, COALESCE(c.months_observed, 0) AS months_with_data, ROUND(s.anomaly_variance::numeric, 4) AS sst_variability, CASE WHEN COALESCE(c.n_profiles, 0) = 0 AND s.anomaly_variance > 1.0 THEN 'blind spot: high uncertainty, zero coverage' WHEN COALESCE(c.n_profiles, 0) < 10 AND s.anomaly_variance > 1.0 THEN 'near-blind: high uncertainty, sparse coverage' WHEN COALESCE(c.n_profiles, 0) > 100 AND s.anomaly_variance < 0.5 THEN 'over-watched: low uncertainty, dense coverage' ELSE 'adequate' END AS coverage_verdict FROM ocean_grid g LEFT JOIN coverage_gap c ON g.lat_bin = c.lat_bin AND g.lon_bin = c.lon_bin LEFT JOIN sst_uncertainty s ON g.lat_bin = s.lat_bin AND g.lon_bin = s.lon_bin ORDER BY sst_variability DESC NULLS LAST LIMIT 30;
The inverse relationship is damning. The ocean grid cells with the highest SST anomaly variance — the places where the climate signal is most volatile and least predictable — are systematically the least instrumented. The South Pacific, the southeastern Atlantic, the Indian Ocean south of the equator: these are the regions where our climate models have the widest uncertainty bounds, and they are precisely the regions where the Argo network has the fewest floats and the buoy network has the fewest stations. The places we know least about are the places we look least at. This is not a paradox. It is a tautology: we know least about what we do not measure, and we do not measure what the deploying nations have no institutional reason to prioritize. The coverage gap is not a gap in technology. It is a gap in political will.

Scenario VI — The Attention Inequality Index

How unequal is the distribution of scientific observation across the ocean — and has the inequality improved or worsened over time?

WITH decadal_obs AS ( -- Combine all observation sources into a unified grid SELECT EXTRACT(DECADE FROM observation_ts) * 10 AS decade, ROUND(lat / 5) * 5 AS lat_bin, ROUND(lon / 5) * 5 AS lon_bin, COUNT(*) AS obs_count FROM argo.float_profiles WHERE quality_flag IN (1, 2) GROUP BY decade, lat_bin, lon_bin UNION ALL SELECT EXTRACT(DECADE FROM observation_ts) * 10, ROUND(lat / 5) * 5, ROUND(lon / 5) * 5, COUNT(*) FROM noaa.buoy_observations GROUP BY 1, 2, 3 UNION ALL SELECT EXTRACT(DECADE FROM observation_date) * 10, ROUND(lat / 5) * 5, ROUND(lon / 5) * 5, COUNT(*) FROM obis.biodiversity_observations GROUP BY 1, 2, 3 ), grid_totals AS ( SELECT decade, lat_bin, lon_bin, SUM(obs_count) AS total_obs FROM decadal_obs GROUP BY decade, lat_bin, lon_bin ), attention_gini AS ( SELECT decade, total_obs, SUM(total_obs) OVER ( PARTITION BY decade ORDER BY total_obs ROWS BETWEEN UNBOUNDED PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW ) AS cumulative_obs, SUM(total_obs) OVER ( PARTITION BY decade ) AS decade_total, COUNT(*) OVER ( PARTITION BY decade ) AS n_cells, ROW_NUMBER() OVER ( PARTITION BY decade ORDER BY total_obs ) AS rank_asc FROM grid_totals ) SELECT decade, n_cells AS ocean_cells_observed, decade_total AS total_observations, -- Gini = 1 - 2 * (area under Lorenz curve) ROUND((1.0 - 2.0 * SUM( cumulative_obs::numeric / decade_total ) / n_cells)::numeric, 4) AS gini_coefficient, CASE WHEN (1.0 - 2.0 * SUM( cumulative_obs::numeric / decade_total ) / n_cells) > 0.7 THEN 'extreme inequality' WHEN (1.0 - 2.0 * SUM( cumulative_obs::numeric / decade_total ) / n_cells) > 0.5 THEN 'high inequality' WHEN (1.0 - 2.0 * SUM( cumulative_obs::numeric / decade_total ) / n_cells) > 0.3 THEN 'moderate inequality' ELSE 'approaching equity' END AS inequality_verdict FROM attention_gini GROUP BY decade, n_cells, decade_total HAVING decade >= 1990 ORDER BY decade;
The Gini coefficient was designed to measure income inequality. Applied to observation density, it measures something equally uncomfortable: the degree to which the scientific community concentrates its instruments in a few privileged ocean regions while ignoring the rest. The optimistic narrative is that Argo, NOAA, and OBIS have expanded ocean coverage dramatically over three decades — more instruments, more nations, more data. The Gini tests whether that expansion actually reduced inequality or merely added more instruments to the regions that were already over-observed. If the coefficient stays flat or rises while total observations grow, the democratization of ocean science is an illusion. The instruments multiplied. The bias did not diversify. More eyes opened, but they opened in the same direction.

Coda

Six measures of scientific attention. One ocean as mirror.

The national gaze reveals whose priorities shape the data. A handful of wealthy nations deploy instruments into every basin, and their observational footprint is not neutral — it is a projection of institutional interests onto the global commons. The taxonomic hierarchy shows which life forms we choose to count, and the hierarchy follows economics and aesthetics, not ecology. The temporal attention cycle shows how funding steers the instruments: the Southern Ocean was invisible until climate politics made it legible; the Arctic emerged only after the ice retreated enough to let ships through. The depth preference shows that accessibility, not importance, determines where we look — the thermocline is more variable than the surface, but the surface is where the buoys sit. The coverage gap proves that the places we know least about are the places we study least, and the correlation is not accidental. The attention inequality index asks whether the expansion of ocean science has reduced or merely rearranged its biases — and the Gini coefficient answers without sentiment.

This paper is the most uncomfortable in the series, because it turns the analytical framework inward. The parallax revealed in Papers 1 through 9 operates on physical systems — temperature fields, current networks, species distributions, embedding distortions. Paper 10 reveals that the parallax operates on us. The observers are not outside the system. They are part of it. Their choices about where to deploy, what to count, and how long to fund the counting are social acts that shape the data as surely as thermodynamics shapes the temperature. The bias is not error. It is structure — institutional structure, national structure, economic structure — and it is perfectly legible in the same SQL tables that describe the ocean itself.

The PARALLAX™ program began with a theorem: any single view of a complex system is mathematically guaranteed to distort. Paper 10 completes the reflexive arc by applying that theorem to the viewing itself. The observation system is a complex system. It has its own biases, its own blind spots, its own scale-dependent artifacts. And the only way to see those biases is to look at the system from outside — which is to say, from another view, which has biases of its own.

There is no view from nowhere. There is only the discipline of documenting where each view stands.

The careful reader will ask what else they focus on.
